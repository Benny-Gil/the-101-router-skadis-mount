What the mount actually is

The part that had to be researched before anything could be drawn.

the101 is a 230 × 230 × 56 mm rounded-square 5G CPE. Its underside has a shallow square well ringed by moulded plastic retention tabs. A flat plate slides in sideways and is captured under those tabs.

There are no screw bosses in the router and no VESA pattern on the router itself. The VESA holes on aftermarket brackets are on the plate, for bolting that plate to a wall or an arm — which is why searching for "the101 VESA" leads nowhere useful.

The clinching detail is the thickness mismatch. The commercial bracket is 5.7 mm thick but the tab slot is only 4.0 mm. So the plate is 4 mm at the edges that pass under the tabs and 5.7 mm across a raised centre pad — which also pins the well depth at about 5.7 mm.

Hang it upside down

The one thing to get right

A slide-in plate needs something to stop it sliding back out. The OEM answer is a detent. The better answer is orientation: mount the bracket so the router is lowered down onto the plate, with the well's open side facing down once it's up.

Gravity then holds the plate seated and no latch is needed at all. To remove the router, lift it.

Three parts

One source file, skadis_101_mount.scad, switched by the part variable.

Bracket

101_skadis_bracket.stl

A 112 × 112 × 2.0 mm plate that slides into the well under the tabs, with six SKÅDIS pegs on the back. This is the real answer.

Fit note. 2.0 mm sits in a 4.0 mm tab slot, so the plate has ~2.0 mm of slop and the router can rock on it slightly. That is the cost of the thin plate, not a bug — raise tongue_t toward 3.8 for a snug fit.

The back carries a perimeter ring and two cross ribs that stand it 9.7 mm off the board — a 104 mm ring inside a 4 mm bare lip, with the cross ribs inboard of it carrying the pegs. That isn't decoration: mounted vertically, the router's vented underside would otherwise sit flat against fibreboard. The ribs buy a 6 mm air gap and, at 2.0 mm, the plate leans on them for most of its stiffness. The pegs run 20.3 mm to reach through.

It's deliberately one flat plate rather than a stepped plate with an oversailing slab: a step would leave an unsupported ledge on the bed.

Keep the layer height a divisor of the plate thickness. At 2.0 mm that means 0.2 mm — ten clean layers. 0.28 mm gives 7.1 and the slicer rounds the plate off spec.

  • Face down, pegs up
  • No supports
  • 3 walls · 25% · 0.2 mm
  • ~28 g
  • ~2 h
  • PETG if it runs warm

Cradle hooks

101_skadis_hooks.stl · print 2

Two L-hooks the router simply sits in. These need no measurements of your unit's well, so they work even if the numbers above turn out to be off. Space them 160 mm apart — four slot columns.

The 62 mm shelf swallows the full 56 mm body depth and the 22 mm lip stops it tipping out. Two pegs 40 mm apart per hook; one peg alone would let the hook swing.

  • On its side
  • Tree supports — pegs only
  • ~19 g each
  • 160 mm apart

Tab gauge

101_skadis_gauge.stl

A comb of 12 mm fingers in 0.2 mm steps from 3.4 to 4.4 mm, plus a 100 mm rule. Slide each finger under one of the router's tabs; the thickest one that goes in without forcing is your real slot gap.

Print this first. Ten minutes and ~10 g, and it derisks the whole bracket. If your gap isn't 4.0 mm, set tongue_t to the measured gap minus 0.2 and re-export.

  • Print first
  • ~10 g
  • ~10 min

Where the weight went

74.2 → 49.6 cm³, ~41 → ~28 g. A third off, at no meaningful cost.

The ribs were three quarters of the part, so that is where the mass was. Two changes did it.

Narrower ribs, 10 → 6 mm

A rib's bending stiffness goes as its height cubed and only linearly with width. The 9.7 mm standoff was always doing the work — 10 mm of width was mass. This drops rib second moment to 60 % of before, against margins that were already ~40×, and 6 mm still leaves 1 mm either side of a peg neck.

A window through the plate's dead centre

The tabs grip the perimeter and the load runs perimeter → ring → cross ribs → pegs, all of it outside the 84 × 62 mm cutout. Nothing structural passed through that area. It vents the well as a bonus. Set lighten = false for the solid plate back.

For more, go after the slicer rather than the model — 2 walls and 15 % infill takes a few more grams. The ribs are only 6 mm wide now, so most of their volume is perimeter either way; the geometry is what saved this.

Tuning

Every dimension is a variable at the top of the .scad file.

Variable Default Change it when
tongue_t 2.0 You want a snug plate instead of the thin one
plate_sq 112.0 Your well measures differently
tongue_fit 0 The plate binds sliding in
peg_clear 0.4 Pegs bind → raise. Sloppy → lower.
board_t 5.2 You have the 3 mm SKÅDIS variant
air_gap 6.0 You want it closer to or further off the board
rib_in 4.0 Wider or narrower lip; also sets the ring's outer size
rib_w 6.0 Below ~5 there's no margin either side of a peg
lighten true You want the solid plate back, no centre window

Will it hold?

A ~1 kg router on six pegs sits well inside both the pegs and a 5 mm SKÅDIS panel. Worst case after lightweighting:

Load pathStressAgainst
Peg neck bending1.3 MPa~50 MPa yield
4 mm lip at a tab0.6 MPa~50 MPa yield
Rib bearing on the board0.003 MPaover 3600 mm²

The perimeter ring bearing flat on the board is what actually takes the tipping moment — the pegs mostly just stop it falling off.
